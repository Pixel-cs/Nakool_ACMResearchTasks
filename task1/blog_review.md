**Prompt Engineering Techniques: Streamlining AI Workflows for Academic Deadlines**

The blog starts with a very relatable situation of your AI assistant messing up and giving vague and unsatisfying answers to even the simplest of questions, whereas at times it can flawlessly explain a complicated technical concept.

The blog then makes an interesting comparison of prompt engineering to an extension of human conversation and asserts that it is not some technical wizardry.

The blog then outlines the process of prompt engineering, including five building blocks: role, task, instructions, context, and input.

From there the article walks us through prompting techniques in increasing complexity. Zero-Shot (instructions with no examples), One-Shot (a single example), and Few-Shot (multiple examples to establish a pattern), and compares their feasibilities in different tasks before moving into more advanced strategies. Chain of Thought (step-by-step reasoning), Role-based prompting (assigning the AI a persona), Contextual Prompting (including a backstory), Retrieval-Augmented Prompting (getting answers from external sources), and Tree of Thought (branching out multiple solution paths before finding the best one). Each technique includes a prompt example alongside a LangChain code snippet, and good infographics for visualization.

**Conclusions**

Reading the blog, and especially the part where it talks about combining various techniques, made me curious as to what combinations of prompting techniques are ideal for a particular task on a particular LLM, and how large is the variance of results in the ideal techniques between different tasks and models and what the reason for this variance is.

i was also curious as to how exactly can we measure the quality of a result for more subjective tasks

My key takeaway from the blog was that prompt engineering is a necessary skill for everyone to learn, and is not very complicated for the value it provides for getting better results with AI.
