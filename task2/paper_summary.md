**Summary: Ten simple rules for reading a scientific paper**

This paper by Carey, Steiner, and Petri published in 2020 argues that reading scientific literature is a distinct skill from reading a novel or textbook, and that most people are never explicitly taught how to do it well.

The authors’ proposed approach starts even before reading the abstract, by looking at the big picture, by identifying our own goal for reading the paper and the authors goal for writing the paper, since they impact our understanding of the paper and help identify biases early on.

From there, rule 3's six questions motivation, methods, context, results, interpretation, and next steps act as a checklist you can apply to the paper as a whole and to each individual figure. Rules 6–8 push readers to stay critical even if the paper is published by a well known author and to stay kind since authors are human, and mistakes often stem from methodological limits rather than incompetence.

The paper closes with the idea that reading isn't complete until you talk about the work and consciously connect it to the existing body of literature using the "Lego brick" metaphor for building on prior research.

**Conclusion**

What I found most useful is Rule 3's six-question framework, mainly because it turns a vague instruction like "read actively" into something concrete and actionable.

What also made me curious was the authors claim that even well established scientific papers by great authors are not etched in stone, which led me to question as to just how often are published research papers in big publications, turn out to be disproven specifically in the field of computer science, so I skimmed through this research paper-[\[2206.06706\] An analysis of retracted papers in Computer Science](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06706)

which concludes that about 8.3% of all retractions from the retraction watch database were for CS.

Although I do think that number of retractions are not the best indicator to knowing how often is CS research disproven.
